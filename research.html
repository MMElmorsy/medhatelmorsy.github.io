Research

We ask numerical models to predict how buildings fail, but we rarely test them against experiments at the scale of a whole structure. My work builds those experiments — and uses what they show to design structures that hold up, including where budgets are smallest.

Benchmark experiments for validating seismic models

Nonlinear building models are calibrated on isolated component tests, then trusted to predict how a complete structure behaves in an earthquake. Almost no experimental data exists at that scale, and none of it is repeated often enough to say anything statistical.

I combine geotechnical centrifuge shake table testing with 3D printing, which makes it feasible to build and test many nominally identical small-scale structures instead of one expensive specimen. The result is a dataset with enough repetitions to support probabilistic validation: not "does the model match this test," but "how much of the scatter in the real structures does the model reproduce?" The unreinforced masonry dataset from this work is openly available, and formed the basis of a blind prediction contest organized with the Pacific Earthquake Engineering Research Center, where independent teams predicted the response before the results were released.

Papers: [J1] benchmark dataset in Earthquake Spectra · [C1], [C4] centrifuge modelling of URM · blind prediction contest results in preparation.

Additive manufacturing for small-scale reinforced concrete

Reduced-scale reinforced concrete has always been limited by the reinforcement. At centrifuge scale you cannot bend and tie a realistic cage, so models end up with reinforcement that is the wrong geometry, the wrong strength, or bonded the wrong way.

I developed additively manufactured steel reinforcement paired with a matched micro-concrete, and characterized it the way any new structural material has to be characterized: tensile response first, then bond, then component behavior. Printed cages were used to build columns and beam-column joints that were tested under cyclic loading and compared against their full-scale counterparts. The outcome is a repeatable production route for small-scale RC specimens, which is what makes statistically meaningful testing programs possible in the first place.

Papers: [J6] tensile and bond behavior · [J4] columns · [J5] beam-column joints · [C2], [C3] centrifuge-scale application.

Seismic topology optimization and robotic prototyping

Current work at MIT, funded by an SNSF Postdoc.Mobility fellowship. Topology optimization has reshaped how we design in steel and in additive metals, but concrete resists it: the material behaves very differently in tension and compression, and seismic demand is dynamic and nonlinear rather than a static load case.

I am developing a bimodular optimization formulation — one that treats concrete as principally a compression material and reinforcement as principally a tension material — driven by earthquake loading. The obstacle is cost: a fully transient nonlinear analysis inside an optimization loop is far too slow, so a large part of the work is finding stiffness-state approximations that keep the physics that matters while making thousands of design iterations tractable. Optimized layouts are then prototyped physically using additive manufacturing and robotic fabrication, so the designs are checked against what can actually be built rather than only against the objective function.

Status: journal paper in preparation. Funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation (CHF 186,666, 2026–2028); host institution MIT.

Affordable earthquake-resistant construction

Most of the world's seismic risk sits in buildings that will never be touched by an engineer. Techniques that assume a design office, a testing lab, and an industrial supply chain do not reach them.

I work on structural systems that stay within the means of low-income regions. One line is corbel dwellings assembled from interlocking compressed earth blocks: blocks that lock together without mortar and can be produced on site from local soil. I characterized their shear behavior and tested complete dwellings on a centrifuge shake table. A second line is low-cost seismic isolation, combining rolling-ball bearings with sand-sandwich layers, tested both in the centrifuge and on a three-directional shake table. The direction I am most interested in is designing the two together, so the isolation system and the superstructure are conceived as one system rather than an expensive technology adapted downward.

Papers: [J2] shear behavior and centrifuge modeling of corbel dwellings · rolling isolation studies in preparation (P2, P3).

Modeling uncertainty and how it propagates

Beam-column joints are among the least constrained parts of a reinforced concrete building model, and the assumptions made there travel all the way to a collapse assessment.

Working from large databases of joint tests, I built probabilistic models of joint behavior and then quantified how that uncertainty affects the seismic response of low-rise RC frames, using record-wise Latin hypercube sampling to keep modeling uncertainty separable from record-to-record variability. The practical question behind it: when a fragility curve shifts, how much of the shift is the building and how much is the modeler?

Papers: [J3] propagation to frame response · [J8], [J9] joint models · [J10], [J11] database reviews.
